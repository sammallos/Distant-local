One day at Sano, four ways to buy it

San Onofre in October. Warm water holding on, the crowd thinned out, and a wave at Old Man's that lets people surf slowly enough to actually look like they're enjoying it. The day has a natural shape — a session ends, a fire gets built, something gets cooked, the light goes — and the options below differ only in how much of that arc we're there for.

Everything is quoted as a flat shoot fee. Crew and talent are separate line items, so you're only paying for the bodies a given option actually needs, and you can see exactly what each one costs. Nothing else appears on the invoice that isn't on this page.

Every figure here is a rate, not an estimate — no hidden line items appear later. Anything billed at cost (permits, lodging, per diems, firewood) is called out as such and approved in writing before it's spent.

Why talent is $300 and not a stipend

A cheaper day rate with a usage ladder on top — organic now, paid later, renewals annually — means someone has to track which face is licensed for what, and you get a phone call in eighteen months asking for more money before you can run an ad. $300 buys the whole thing on the day. Shoot it, use it anywhere, forever, and never think about it again. For a brand building a library, that's worth far more than the $150 it saves.

04 / Direction

Cozy, but cold at the edges

The whole look lives on one tension: warm light against cold water. Firelight on a face, dark surf behind it. Wool over neoprene. Steam off a pan into wind. If a frame doesn't have both temperatures in it, it's not the shot.

Light

  • Sun under 15° for everything before the fire — long shadows, backlit spray, flare through a salt-dusted front element.
  • Once the sun's down, the fire is the only key. No LED panels pretending to be firelight. If it needs a lift, it's a bounce card or a hurricane lantern that's in frame anyway.
  • Underexpose the ambient a stop past sunset so faces read hot and the ocean goes deep blue-black.
  • Shoot into the dark until the fire is genuinely the brightest thing in the frame. That's the money hour.

Texture & wardrobe

  • Heavy knits pulled on over a half-peeled wetsuit. Beanies. A wool blanket over the shoulders.
  • Waxed canvas, cast iron, driftwood, enamel mugs, a battered cooler.
  • Bare feet in cold sand. Wet hair. Goosebumps. Nobody looks comfortable and that's the point.
  • Product is worn, not presented — sandy, damp, half-zipped, the way it looks on day two hundred.

Casting note

Real surfers and real friends, not models. People who can actually get a fire going and don't flinch at cold water. The camera should feel like a fourth person at the fire, not a crew of six pointed at two people. Nobody smiles at the lens.

Anti-references

No glossy studio product. No drone-heavy travel-influencer edit. No orange-and-teal grade pushed past believable. No music-video pacing — this is a slow, patient piece that earns its warmth.

05 / Treatment

Last Light — the film

Logline: An afternoon at Old Man's ends the way the best ones do — a fire on the sand, a pan of something hot, and the last hour of light shared with people who stayed in the water too long.

No voiceover. No hard sell. The product is worn through every frame and never announced. The film's job is to make someone watching in November want to be cold and outside with their friends, wearing this.

The arc

ArriveTruck doors. Boards off the rack. Checking the water from the top of the dune. Almost no dialogue — just wind and the sound of gear.
Suit upZips, wax, the tailgate ritual. Detail frames where product does its actual work.

EatPlates on knees. Enamel mugs. Overheads of the food on a blanket. Real conversation, mostly unusable and entirely necessary for the sound bed.

06 / Logistics

San Onofre, and the one thing that has to be solved first

Sano is the right beach for this — the wave suits the tone, the bluff gives a clean backdrop, and the crowd thins out in October. It also has the tightest rules of anywhere on this stretch of coast, and they land squarely on the two hours the concept needs.

The fire question

San Onofre Surf Beach day-use closes at sunset, and beach fires are not permitted on the sand there. That is precisely the window from fire-lit through embers — blocks 5 to 8, which is most of what makes this film worth making.

My understanding of the workable versions, in order of preference:

  • Book a site at the Bluffs campground. Campsites have fire rings and a legal reason to be there after dark. We shoot the session on the beach in daylight, then move up to the site for the fire and the cook. Costs a night's camping and is the cleanest fix.
  • Split the location. Session and come-in-cold at Sano in the light, fire and cook at a beach with fire rings — Doheny, Calafia, or San Clemente pier are all close. Two locations in one day, which Option C already budgets for.
  • Lose the fire. Camp stove and hurricane lanterns on the sand before the gate closes. Smaller flame, same warmth, and it still works — but it isn't the film in the treatment.

Treat all of the above as needing confirmation. State Parks rules on this stretch change, campground availability changes, and I would rather call the park and be told I'm wrong now than find out on the day. This is the first phone call, before we pick a date.

Before we lock a date

  • Confirm the fire plan with State Parks and book the campsite if that's the route. Everything else depends on it.
  • Tide. A low-to-mid outgoing tide through the afternoon gives dry sand to work on and a wide beach in the wides. Old Man's also works better on the push.
  • Swell and wind. Small and clean is genuinely better here than big. Santa Ana offshores in October are the ideal — glassy water, warm air, and the wind holding the smoke off the lens.
  • Date window. Mid-to-late October. Water still holding warmth, sunset still around 6:15p, crowds thin midweek. After the clocks change it's a different, much shorter day.
  • Burn ban status checked the week of, and again 24 hrs out.

On the day

  • Day-use fee per vehicle at the gate. Budget two vehicles for Option C, three for D. Carpool where possible — parking on the bluff road is linear and fills.
  • Everything gets carried down the trail. Firewood, cast iron, cooler, and grip in one trip if we plan the load properly, two if we don't.
  • Buy firewood locally. Don't transport wood in — it's both a rule and a pest issue.
  • The SONGS domes sit to the north and are visible from parts of the beach. They're either a deliberate composition or carefully framed out — no accidental nuclear plant in a cozy autumn film.
  • No drone. Camp Pendleton airspace and the SONGS site. Not worth the conversation.

Safety, non-negotiable

  • Water safety person on set for any in-surf shooting. Nobody goes in alone with a housing.
  • Bucket, shovel, and water on site before the first match. Fire drowned and stirred at wrap, not buried.
  • Headlamps for every person for load-out. The trail is dark and uneven.
  • Warm layers and hot drinks for talent between takes — cold, unhappy people read on camera immediately.
  • Leave-no-trace sweep. We're guests, and I'd like to be able to shoot there again.

Weather & fallbacks

  • Go/no-go called 48 hrs out, together.
  • One named backup date held from the start. Moving to it costs nothing.
  • Overcast is fine — often better. Hard rain or a burn ban is the only real no-go.
  • Flat surf — the session becomes a paddle-out and a walk. The arc doesn't depend on a good wave, which is part of why Sano is the right call.
  • Costs already spent on food, wood, or camping when we postpone carry to the new date rather than being re-billed.
